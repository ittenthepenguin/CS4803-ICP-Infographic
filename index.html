The customer

1. The Ideal Customer Profile: Claims and SIU Personnel at Commercial-Auto Carriers

Our customer decides whether evidence in a commercial-auto claim can be trusted. In practice that is a VP or Director of Claims with a Special Investigations Unit reporting to her, at a U.S. property-and-casualty carrier writing commercial auto. We chose her because insurers are the only party in this chain who already buy protection against losses that have not happened yet.

A

Who she is

Demographics

Her book runs anywhere from regional to national, and her team works in Guidewire ClaimCenter or CCC. At larger carriers the SIU director sits one rung below her, so both belong in any conversation we have.

Her line of business loses money on liability and only on liability. Commercial-auto liability posted a 113 combined ratio in 2024, close to 113.3 the year before, and has stayed above 100 every year since 2018, while physical damage came in at 88.6 and has not crossed 100 since 2017 (AM Best via Insurance Journal). The damage sits precisely in the claims where video decides who pays. The overall line hit 105.4 in 2022 on a $3.3 billion underwriting loss, over half of it from adverse development on older accident years (AM Best via Insurance Journal).

Her anti-fraud budget is a legal obligation. New York requires carriers to maintain an SIU and file a fraud-prevention plan under Section 409 of its insurance law (NY DFS), and most states impose a version of the same duty.

What shapes her decisions

Psychographics

She is graded on the losses she failed to stop, which pulls her in one direction. A fraudulent payment lands in her loss ratio with her name on it, while an over-investigated legitimate claim costs staff hours nobody audits. That imbalance is why her team works marginal files harder than efficiency would justify, and why a control that shortens investigations without raising exposure gets a real hearing.

What frightens her is the tail, not the average. Nuclear verdicts against trucking companies, meaning awards above $10 million, exceeded $5.5 billion between 2013 and 2022 before appeals, roughly 90 percent of them in state courts (U.S. Chamber Institute for Legal Reform). Across 154 trucking cases, the mean plaintiffs' verdict was $31.9 million against a median of $314,217 (FleetOwner on the ILR study). That gap between mean and median is the thing she is defending against, and evidence that survives cross-examination is the only armor she controls.

She reads fraud as theft from honest policyholders. Fraud across all lines of insurance, life and health and property-casualty together, is estimated at $308.6 billion a year (Coalition Against Insurance Fraud), but the number that describes her own book is narrower: about 10 percent of property-and-casualty claims are estimated fraudulent, roughly $122 billion annually (Deloitte). We keep those figures in separate lanes, because only the smaller one is hers. The argument she uses to defend her budget is the FBI estimate that fraud adds $400 to $700 a year to the average family's premiums (via Deloitte).

She has been sold AI before and has gotten selective. Claims and SIU leaders named keeping pace with technology, closing the talent gap, and countering new fraud schemes as their leading concerns (Verisk), and anti-fraud headcount has grown slowly while referral volume climbed (Coalition Against Insurance Fraud, 2024 SIU Benchmarking Study). She cannot hire her way out of a rising caseload, which is the honest reason automation reaches her desk at all.

What she does today

Behavioral Insights

She already requests fleet video, and it arrives in whatever shape the fleet's vendor produces. ClaimCenter then stores it as a document, the same object class as a PDF or a photograph, with no first-class field for a hash, a camera identity, or a custody record (Guidewire documentation). Provenance survives in her files as a habit, not as data.

Every fleet hands over footage under different rules. Motive caps recall clips at one to three minutes and rate-limits requests to ten per minute under a monthly quota, while Samsara queues the request on the device and delivers whenever the truck is next online (Samsara support documentation). Lytx and Netradyne publish separate interfaces again. Any one fleet learns one set of quirks. She inherits all of them. developer.lytx

She is buying in this cycle. Thirty-five percent of insurance executives put fraud detection in their top five areas for generative-AI investment over the following year (Deloitte), and Verisk already sells manipulation detection into her core system as a ClaimCenter accelerator. The budget exists and money is moving through it. Her purchasing runs through security review, integration requirements, and a single-line pilot before anything scales. marketplace.guidewire

The strategic case

2. Strategic Justification: Why Insurance Personnel, and Why Now

They buy prevention, which is what this is

Pricing and reducing losses that have not happened yet is her job, so a control protecting future claims fits how she already spends. Everyone else in the chain buys something different. A fleet buys equipment and hopes for a premium break, a TPA buys throughput, a camera vendor sells hardware and storage. Verifying the origin of evidence is a preventive control, and she holds the only mandated budget for preventive controls in this chain (NY DFS).

That also settles what we sell. Detection asks whether a file was manipulated, and Verisk already answers that inside her platform. Provenance asks where a clip was born, whether it changed, and who touched it. Detection reacts to a suspicious file already in the system. Provenance makes a whole class of forgery unusable before anyone has to catch it, which is the posture she is paid to take. marketplace.guidewire

Why now

The tools changed faster than the claim file.

Faking evidence stopped requiring skill. Ninety-eight percent of insurers agree AI editing tools are driving an increase in digital insurance fraud, 76 percent say manipulated submissions have grown more sophisticated, and 36 percent of consumers, rising to 55 percent of Generation Z, would consider digitally altering a claim image or document even knowing it breaks the rules (Verisk State of Insurance Fraud, March 2026, surveying 1,000 U.S. consumers and 300 claims professionals). The people submitting evidence hold better tools than the people judging it.

Legislatures moved before insurers did. Forty-six states have passed deepfake legislation since 2022 and the federal TAKE IT DOWN Act became law in May 2025 (Jones Walker), and the NAIC's Antifraud Task Force is tracking new state laws on AI, deepfakes, forged digital likeness, and synthetic identity (NAIC minutes, August 2026).

Meanwhile states started pushing more video into claims on purpose. Louisiana now requires qualifying commercial-auto insurers to offer an actuarially justified liability-premium discount for vehicles carrying a dashboard camera and telematics, and the statute demands compatibility between the two for data verification. Regulators are paying fleets to produce more of the evidence that just became cheap to fake. legis.la

Documented fraud today is altered photos and documents, and we will not claim forged crash video is already common, because the evidence does not show it. That is the point for a preventive buyer. Once video becomes the most trusted item in a claim file, and Louisiana is already discounting premiums to generate more of it, video becomes the most valuable item to forge. The gap between today's photo fraud and tomorrow's video fraud is the only window in which prevention is worth buying.

One honest caveat

She is also the hardest person for three students to reach, and fleet safety directors will answer sooner. So our interview plan and our customer hypothesis are different things. We interview across the ecosystem and name the carrier as the buyer. If interviews show the cost of bad evidence actually lands on the fleet or the TPA, the ICP moves.

The people around the customer

3. Key Stakeholder Map

Five groups can make this work or kill it, and none of them is the customer.

01

Insured commercial fleets own the cameras and physically hold the footage. Their safety director is the one pulling a clip after a driver calls in a collision. What they want is to be cleared when they were not at fault, pay less next renewal, and add nothing to a thin safety team. Louisiana's discount statute gives them money riding on whether their camera data is verifiable. Their influence is high on whether this is feasible and low on whether anyone buys it, because nothing reaches the carrier without them and they can quietly refuse any process that adds a step. They are also the people we can actually get on the phone, and the ones whose day we understand least. legis.la

02

Third-party administrators run claims for self-insured fleets and for carriers. Broadspire, whose parent Crawford & Company is headquartered in Atlanta, took in 1.49 million cases in 2025. They care about cycle time, clean subrogation, and files that stay closed, and because they receive evidence from many fleets they probably feel the same fragmentation our customer does. Their influence is high, and they are the group most likely to prove us wrong, since they could be a channel, a second segment, or the buyer we should have picked. That is exactly why we interview them. stocktitan

03

Claims platforms and camera vendors own the pipes. Guidewire and CCC on one end, Samsara and Motive and Lytx and Netradyne on the other. They want to keep their customers, control their own integration surface, and not answer support tickets for someone else's product, which is why CCC gates third-party developer registration on Secure Share (CCC Secure Share). Their influence is the highest of any group here and partly adversarial, because they set the quotas, the clip limits, and the marketplace terms. Guidewire has already published its own position on AI-generated media fraud and hosts Verisk's forensics tool, so they could open a door for us, absorb the idea, or make a middleman pointless. We do not know which yet. guidewire

04

Defense counsel and SIU investigators decide what evidence is actually worth. They want proof that holds when the other side attacks it. Under the federal rules, a record produced by an electronic process can be authenticated through a qualified person's certification, following the form of Rule 902(11) and with advance written notice to the other party (Federal Rule of Evidence 902). Their influence is high on requirements and nonexistent on budget. Software can build the technical record that certifier relies on, but it cannot be the certifier, cannot serve the notice, and does not make anything automatically admissible, since relevance and hearsay still apply and state courts run their own rules.

05

Drivers and labor representatives are the ones being recorded. Most accept a road-facing camera that can clear them and far fewer accept a microphone over their seat. California Penal Code section 632 penalizes recording a confidential communication without all-party consent and makes those recordings inadmissible, with an exception where people could reasonably expect to be overheard. That covers in-cab audio rather than road video. Their influence is medium and lands where it hurts most, because consent handled badly stalls adoption no matter what the carrier signed. law.justia

Where this leads

4. Summary: Connecting the ICP and Stakeholders to Our Mission and Opportunity

Our mission

Our mission is that every commercial-auto claim gets decided on incident video that is real, complete, and traceable to the vehicle that recorded it.

Naming insurance personnel as the customer follows from one fact. They are the only group here who already buy protection against future losses as a matter of business model and law (NY DFS). Provenance is a preventive control, so it maps onto a budget she already defends instead of asking her to invent a category.

The stakeholder map is why this is a coordination problem rather than a feature. Fleets hold the footage but cannot set a standard, platforms hold the integration but sell to a different buyer, counsel sets the bar but controls no money, and drivers hold a working veto over consent. Carriers are the only participant who can require an intake standard from everyone submitting evidence to them, which is why a standard has to be pulled by the receiver instead of pushed by the source.

Timing is what makes this a company and not a 2019 feature request. Generative AI made evidence cheap to fabricate (Verisk) in the same stretch that states began paying fleets to produce more of it and 46 states wrote deepfake law (Jones Walker). legis.la

Every claim here is a hypothesis with a date on it, and fifteen interviews will tell us what to throw away.
